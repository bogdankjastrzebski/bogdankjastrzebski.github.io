# The Greatest Informational Lower Bound: How Category Theory Defines the Minimal Sufficient Statistic

In statistics, we are obsessed with **data reduction**. If you flip a coin 1,000 times, you don’t need to record the exact sequence of heads and tails; you just need the total number of heads. The rest is noise.

When a statistic captures all the relevant information about an underlying parameter without losing a single drop of signal, we call it a **Sufficient Statistic**. But there’s a catch: there are *infinitely many* sufficient statistics for any given problem. The raw data itself is trivially sufficient. The sorted data is sufficient.

Naturally, statisticians want the absolute most compressed version: the **Minimal Sufficient Statistic**. It is the ultimate informational bottleneck. But how do we rigorously define "minimal" without relying on clunky, case-by-case algebra?

This is where the beautiful abstraction of **Category Theory** steps in. By viewing information categorically, the Minimal Sufficient Statistic reveals itself to be exactly one thing: **a categorical limit**.

### The Category of Information

To connect these worlds, we have to define "information" mathematically. In rigorous probability theory, a statistic generates a $\sigma$-algebra. You can think of a $\sigma$-algebra as the exact boundary of what a statistic "knows."

Imagine a category where the objects are these $\sigma$-algebras (bodies of information).

* We draw an arrow from object $A$ to object $B$ if $A \subseteq B$.
* In data terms, $\sigma(T_1) \subseteq \sigma(T_2)$ means statistic $T_1$ is a *data reduction* of $T_2$. $T_1$ contains less (or equal) information.

Because there is at most one arrow between any two objects in this setup, this category forms a **Partially Ordered Set (Poset)**.

### The Categorical Limit as an "Informational Lower Bound"

In Category Theory, the **limit** of a diagram is essentially the "greatest lower bound" of that system.

Let's build a diagram consisting of *all* possible sufficient statistics for our model.

1. **An "Informational Lower Bound":** This would be a statistic that can be computed *from* any other sufficient statistic. In our Poset, it’s an object that sits below all the others, meaning it has thrown away as much (or more) noise than any of them.
2. **The "Greatest" Informational Lower Bound:** We want the bound that throws away all the noise, but *stops* exactly before it throws away the signal. In a Poset of $\sigma$-algebras, the greatest lower bound of a collection of objects is simply their **intersection**.

So, categorically, the limit of all sufficient information is:


$$\mathcal{T}_{min} = \bigcap_{S \in \text{Sufficient}} \sigma(S)$$

### Bahadur's Theorem: The Magic Trick

This categorical construct is elegant, but it begs a massive question: If we take the intersection (the categorical limit) of all these sufficient $\sigma$-algebras, is the resulting intersection *itself* still a sufficient statistic? Or did we destroy the signal by intersecting them?

In 1954, the statistician R.R. Bahadur proved the missing link. Bahadur's Theorem states that for any dominated statistical model (which includes almost all models we use in the real world), **the intersection of all sufficient $\sigma$-algebras is, in fact, sufficient.**

### The Grand Conclusion

Bahadur’s theorem perfectly closes the loop between frequentist statistics and algebraic topology.

The Minimal Sufficient Statistic isn't just a clever algebraic trick used to simplify equations. It is strictly the **categorical limit** of the entire system of information. It is the greatest informational lower bound—the terminal destination where all irrelevant noise is stripped away, leaving only the pure, uncut geometry of the likelihood function.
